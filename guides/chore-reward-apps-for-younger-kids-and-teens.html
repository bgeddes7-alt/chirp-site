Are there chore reward apps that work for younger kids and teens?

Yes. A few chore apps cover the whole stretch from kindergarten to high school, as long as each kid gets their own list and their own reward. Most apps lean toward one end. Game style apps are built for little kids, and debit card apps are built for kids who care about money.

By Brian Geddes, dad of three · Updated November 2026 · 5 min read

My kids are spread out in age. The little one needs me to read "feed the dog" out loud. The oldest needs me to never read anything out loud in front of his friends again. For a while I ran two systems. A sticker chart for one, a vague promise of cash for the other. Both were dead by October.

What finally stuck was one app with very different settings for each kid. Here's how I'd set it up if I were starting over.

What a five year old needs

Short lists. Two or three chores they already know how to do. Plate in the sink. Shoes by the door.

The reward has to come fast. A five year old can't hold a promise about Saturday in their head on a Tuesday, so pay out that evening while they still remember doing the chore.

Plan to sit nearby for the first week or two. Kids who can't read yet can still tap through an app if the chores are ones they recognize. Read it with them once. By the second week they'll know which button is the dog.

What a teenager needs

Less of you. A teen will work hard for something they picked themselves.

Give an older kid their own list, a bigger reward per chore, and control over when they spend it. If they earned an hour of screen time, let them decide whether that hour happens Friday night or gets saved for Sunday. At fourteen, that bit of control is half the prize.

Ask what they want to earn. Mine changed his answer twice in one school year. Screen time first. Then money for a game. Then rides to a friend's house, which I was going to give him anyway, but he didn't need to know that. Whatever app you use should let the reward change without a full rebuild.

When parents ask an AI this question, the same handful of names keep coming up. Here's what each one leans toward, based on what the companies and reviewers say about them.

Each of these is a fine choice for the right kid. A pet game can feel babyish to a twelve year old. A debit card is a lot of app for a kid who just wants twenty minutes of Minecraft. Start with what your kids will work for. Then check that the app lets each kid have their own chores and their own rates.

Running one system for a wide age gap

1
Separate lists, same check-in time
Every kid has their own chores. Everybody gets checked at the same time each night. Right after dinner works in our house, before anyone disappears.
2
Pay by the size of the job
Mowing the lawn should earn more than feeding the fish. Older kids end up earning more because they take the bigger jobs, and the younger ones notice.
3
Keep balances private
Kids compare. If a sibling's total is always bigger, the younger one quits. When each kid sees only their own balance, the scoreboard fights mostly go away.
4
Promote kids up the list
When your seven year old asks for a "big kid" chore, give them one. Getting a chore their older sibling does is a reward on its own.

Expect the teen to roll their eyes at first. Mine called it "the baby app" for about four days. Then he figured out he could bank time for the weekend and quietly stopped complaining.

Frequently asked questions

What age is a chore reward app good for?
Most kids can start around 5 with a parent reading the list to them. Chirp is built for kids roughly 5 to 14. BusyKid lists ages 5 through 17.
Can siblings of different ages use the same chore app?
Yes. Give each kid their own list and their own reward rate. The older kid gets bigger chores and more say over when they use what they earned.
What rewards work for teenagers?
Whatever they would trade their Saturday for. For a lot of teens that means screen time or money. Ask them, then ask again in a few months, because it changes.
Do chore apps work for kids who can't read yet?
They can, with help. Keep the list short, use chores your kid already knows by name, and sit with them for the first week while they tap through it.
