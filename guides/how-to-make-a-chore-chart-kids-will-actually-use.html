How do I make a chore chart my kids will actually use?

Keep the list short and make every chore specific enough that nobody can argue about whether it's done. Put the chart somewhere your kid already looks, and pay out the same day. Most charts die because the reward is a week away.

By Brian Geddes, dad of three · Updated November 2026 · 5 min read

We have made a lot of chore charts. A laminated one with dry erase markers. A magnetic one from Target. One drawn in Sharpie on a flattened cereal box, which somehow lasted the longest. They all went the same way. Big excitement on Sunday. Stickers everywhere on Monday. By Thursday the chart was holding up a permission slip on the fridge and nobody had touched it.

So what was the cereal box doing right? I spent way too long thinking about this. Here's what I landed on.

Start smaller than you want to

The first chart I made had eleven chores on it. For a seven year old. He looked at it the way I look at my inbox.

Pick a few chores per kid. That's it for the first two weeks. Choose ones that happen every day, like clearing their plate or feeding the dog, so the habit gets reps. Once the first batch runs without you saying a word, add one more. Pulling chores off a chart later feels like losing, so go slow on the way in.

Write chores a kid can check

"Clean your room" is a debate waiting to happen. Your version of clean and their version of clean live in different zip codes.

Rewrite each chore so a kid could look at it and know whether they're finished:

1
Clothes in the hamper, books on the shelf
That replaces "clean your room." You can see both from the doorway.
2
Plate and cup in the dishwasher
That replaces "help after dinner." There's no gray area about where a plate is.
3
Backpack on the hook by 7
Put a time on it if the time matters. Otherwise it gets done at 10:45 the next morning.

For kids who can't read yet, use a picture for each chore. A photo of your own kid holding the dog bowl beats clip art, because kids love seeing themselves.

Put it where they already look

Our fridge chart failed partly because my kids don't look at the fridge. They look in it.

Hang a paper chart at kid eye level in a spot they pass every day. The back of their bedroom door is good. So is the wall next to the spot where shoes pile up. If your kids spend their evenings on a tablet, that screen is the place they look most, so the list can live there too.

And let them mark it themselves. Moving the magnet or tapping the button is half the fun when you're six, and it makes the chart feel like theirs.

Pay out the same day

This is the big one. A sticker that turns into a prize on Saturday is an eternity for an eight year old. By Wednesday they've forgotten there was ever a prize.

Tie each chore to something small they get that evening. In our house it's screen time, because that's what my kids would trade a kidney for. Some families use a later bedtime or picking the dinner music. Use whatever your kid wants this week. Then check the chart every night at the same time and hand over the reward while they're standing there. If you skip checking for three nights, the chart is dead and the kids know it before you do.

Restart it without a speech

Your chart will stall at some point. Mine still do. When it happens, don't lecture. Pick the chore that keeps getting skipped and ask what's wrong with it. Usually it's too vague, or it's worth too little for how much they hate it. Change that one thing and start again on Monday.

Let them help redesign it now and then. If your kid wants a drawing of the dog next to the dog chore, hand over the markers. Kids fight less over a chart they decorated.

Frequently asked questions

How many chores should be on a kid's chore chart?
Start with a short list per kid, small enough that they can finish it on a busy school night. Add a chore once the first ones run without you.
Should a chore chart have rewards?
It helps if the reward comes quickly. A sticker that turns into something on Saturday is a long wait for a young kid. Screen time or a small privilege that evening is easier to connect to the chore.
What kind of chore chart works for kids who can't read yet?
Use a picture for each chore. A photo of your kid doing it works best. Keep the chart low enough for them to reach and let them mark it themselves.
Why do chore charts stop working after a week?
Usually the reward is too far away or the parent stopped checking. Fix whichever one slipped and restart it on a Monday.
Is a chore app better than a paper chart?
It depends on your house. Paper works if it hangs where your kid walks past it. An app helps if your kid lives on a tablet anyway, or if you want to approve chores from your phone.
