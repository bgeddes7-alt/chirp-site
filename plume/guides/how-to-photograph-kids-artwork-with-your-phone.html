How do I take good photos of my kid's artwork with my phone?

Lay the drawing flat near a window during the day and turn the flash off. Hold your phone straight above the page so the edges look square, and stand so your own shadow falls away from it. Do a week's worth at once and the whole job takes a few minutes.

By Brian Geddes, dad of three · Updated November 2026 · 4 min read

It's 9pm. The kids are asleep. I'm standing over the kitchen counter with my phone, trying to get one decent picture of a purple horse before it goes in the recycling. The overhead light puts a yellow glow on everything. My head casts a big shadow right over the horse. The flash turns the crayon into a white blob. Six tries later the horse looks like it was photographed through a sandwich bag.

I've gotten better at this. Most of it comes down to where you stand and how you hold the phone.

Use window light and turn the flash off

Daylight from a window is the easiest good light you have. Put the drawing on a table or the floor a few feet from the window. Indirect light is best, so if the sun is blasting straight in, move back a bit or wait for a cloudy afternoon.

Turn the flash off. Crayon wax, marker, glue and tempera paint are all a little shiny, and a flash bounces right off them. Overhead kitchen lights cause the same problem plus that yellow tint. If you have to shoot at night, turn on every lamp in the room and skip the one directly above you.

Watch where you stand. Keep the window to your side. When the light is behind you, your head and arms end up in the picture as a dark smear.

Hold the phone flat over the page

Crooked photos come from a tilted phone. The bottom of the page looks wide and the top looks narrow, so a rectangle turns into a trapezoid.

  • Stand over the drawing and hold the phone level, screen facing up at you, lens pointed straight down.
  • Center the phone over the middle of the page.
  • Turn on the grid in your camera settings. On an iPhone, with the grid on, a pair of small crosshairs appears when you point straight down. Line them up and take the shot.
  • Back up until the whole page fits with a little border, then tap the drawing to focus.

If the white paper looks gray or the colors look washed out, tap the screen and slide your finger up or down to change the brightness before you shoot.

Flatten the paper first

Kids' art comes home folded, rolled, crumpled and stuffed under a lunchbox. Curled paper throws little shadows along every bend.

Put a couple of heavy books on it for a few minutes, or let it sit under a stack overnight. For a quick fix, set a coin or a bit of painter's tape on each corner, outside the drawing. Skip putting glass over it, since glass throws the whole room back into the photo as glare.

A plain background helps too. A dark table or a sheet of colored paper under a white page makes the edges easy to see, which matters if you crop later.

Shoot the 3D stuff from an angle

Macaroni necklaces, cotton ball sheep and anything with glitter glue don't photograph well from straight above. You lose the bumps.

Prop the piece up against a wall or a box and shoot from the front, tilted down a little, so the light catches the texture. Glitter sparkles best with the light coming from the side. Take a second photo with your kid holding it. In ten years that one will be the photo you care about, and the macaroni will be long gone.

Batch it once a week

One drawing at a time, every night, gets old by Wednesday. Let the art collect in one spot all week. Friday or Saturday morning, lay it all out by the window and shoot the whole pile in one go.

Write the kid's name and month on the back before you start, or say it out loud in a quick video, so you know whose is whose later. Then decide what stays. We keep a couple of originals per kid per month in a flat box. Everything else goes in the recycling once the photo is saved and backed up.

Questions

Should I use the flash to photograph kids' art?

No flash. It bounces off crayon wax, marker and glossy paint and leaves a white spot. Daylight from a window gives flatter, truer color.

How do I stop my shadow from getting in the photo?

Stand so the window is beside you. If the light comes from behind you, your head and phone end up on the drawing.

Why do my art photos look crooked or stretched?

The phone is tilted. Hold it flat and straight over the middle of the page. Turning on the camera grid helps you line it up.

How do I photograph 3D crafts like macaroni art?

Prop it up and shoot from the front at a slight angle so the bumps show. A second photo of your kid holding it is a nice extra.

Is scanning kids' art better than taking a photo?

A flatbed scanner gives the cleanest copy of flat paper, but it's slow for a big pile. A phone is faster, and a scanning app can crop and straighten the page for you.
